01
THE DAWN OF THE ATOMIC AGE

History with plot twists

Open each file to see how one discovery unlocked the next.

1896

Becquerel discovers radioactivity

Uranium salts fog photographic plates even without sunlight — evidence that atoms can release radiation spontaneously.

1898

Marie & Pierre Curie push radioactivity forward

The Curies isolated and studied highly radioactive materials, helping turn radioactivity into a serious field of physics.

1911

Rutherford reveals the nucleus

The gold-foil experiment showed that most of the atom is empty space and nearly all positive charge is concentrated in a tiny nucleus.

1938–1939

Fission is discovered and explained

Experiments by Otto Hahn and Fritz Strassmann produced unexpectedly light products from uranium. Lise Meitner and Otto Frisch explained the nucleus had split.

1942

Chicago Pile-1 reaches sustained criticality

Enrico Fermi’s team demonstrated the first controlled, self-sustaining chain reaction on 2 December 1942.

1950s → now

From experimental piles to power reactors

Reactor engineering evolved into commercial PWRs and BWRs, then Gen III/III+ designs and today’s advanced-reactor and SMR programmes.

02
CORE MECHANICS

What actually happens in fission?

Less memorising. More cause-and-effect.

⚛

1. A neutron arrives

U-235 can absorb a neutron. The resulting excited nucleus is unstable.

〰️

2. The nucleus deforms

Nuclear forces and electric repulsion compete as the excited nucleus stretches.

💥

3. The nucleus splits

Two main fission fragments form, together with radiation and typically a few neutrons.

🔥

4. Energy becomes heat

The fragments fly apart with high kinetic energy; collisions in the fuel turn much of that motion into thermal energy.

WHY ENERGY IS RELEASED

Think “more tightly bound,” not “mass disappears.”

Medium-mass nuclei are generally more tightly bound per nucleon than very heavy nuclei. After a heavy nucleus splits, the products can have a slightly lower total mass-energy. That difference appears as released energy, consistent with E = mc².

k < 1

Subcritical

Each generation causes fewer fissions. The reaction dies away.

k ≈ 1

Critical

A steady self-sustaining chain reaction. “Critical” does not mean “about to explode.”

k > 1

Supercritical

Fission population rises from one generation to the next.

PWR

Pressurized Water Reactor

Primary water is kept at high pressure so it does not boil in the reactor vessel. It transfers heat to a separate secondary loop that makes steam.

BWR

Boiling Water Reactor

Water boils directly in the reactor vessel. Steam from the reactor goes to the turbine, then is condensed and reused.

NUCLEAR CASE FILES

When physics meets engineering reality

Accidents are studied not for shock value, but because they reveal how feedback, heat removal and safety layers matter.

CASE 1986CHERNOBYL UNIT 4
steam ↑

Physics focus: positive void feedback

In the RBMK conditions present during the accident, increasing steam voids could increase reactivity. More reactivity could raise power, creating more boiling and still more voids — a dangerous positive feedback loop.

More steam voids→higher reactivity→higher power→more boiling

Lesson: reactor feedback coefficients and control-system design can determine whether a disturbance damps itself or grows.

Physics focus: decay heat does not instantly vanish

Reactors shut down after the earthquake, but radioactive fission products continued to generate decay heat. Tsunami flooding caused severe power and cooling losses, making heat removal extremely difficult.

Shutdown≠zero heat→cooling still needed

Lesson: reliable decay-heat removal, backup power, diverse cooling paths and protection from extreme hazards are essential.

🛡️

Defence in depth

Modern nuclear safety uses multiple layers: prevention, control, cooling, containment, monitoring, emergency procedures and independent regulation. No single barrier should carry the entire safety burden.

05
THE HORIZON

Next-generation fission

Advanced concepts explore different coolants, neutron spectra, fuel forms and deployment sizes.

SMR

Small Modular Reactors

Smaller units intended to support modular construction and flexible deployment.

MSR

Molten Salt Reactors

Use molten salts as coolant and, in some concepts, as part of the fuel system.

FAST

Fast Reactors

Operate without a neutron moderator and may use liquid-metal coolants such as sodium or lead.

HTGR

High-Temperature Gas Reactors

Use gas cooling and high-temperature fuel systems that can support electricity and industrial heat applications.

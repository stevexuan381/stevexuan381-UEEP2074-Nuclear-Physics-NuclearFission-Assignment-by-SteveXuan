01
THE ATOMIC AGE

How we discovered the nuclear world

Modern nuclear physics grew through a chain of experiments that changed the way scientists understood matter.

Core idea: the atom is not indivisible

By the late nineteenth century, atoms were often treated as the smallest building blocks of matter. Radioactivity, the electron and the atomic nucleus showed that atoms contain smaller structures and can undergo nuclear change.

1896

Becquerel — radioactivity

Henri Becquerel found that uranium compounds could affect photographic plates without an external light source. The radiation came from the material itself.

1898

Marie and Pierre Curie

The Curies investigated radioactive substances and discovered polonium and radium, helping establish radioactivity as an important area of physics.

1911

Rutherford — the nucleus

The gold-foil experiment showed that atoms are mostly empty space, while positive charge and most of the mass are concentrated in a tiny nucleus.

Study link: Large-angle alpha scattering is evidence for a small, dense, positively charged nucleus.
1932

Chadwick — the neutron

James Chadwick identified the neutron. Because the neutron has no electric charge, it can approach nuclei without being repelled by positive nuclear charge.

1938–1939

Fission is discovered and explained

Hahn and Strassmann observed unexpectedly light products after bombarding uranium with neutrons. Lise Meitner and Otto Frisch explained that the uranium nucleus had split.

1942

Chicago Pile-1

Enrico Fermi's team achieved the first controlled, self-sustaining nuclear chain reaction. This demonstrated that a chain reaction could be managed rather than simply allowed to grow.

1950s →

From experiments to electricity

Reactor technology developed into commercial power stations using controlled fission heat to produce steam and drive electrical generators.

Why was the neutron so important?

A neutron has no electric charge, so it can enter a positively charged nucleus more easily than a proton. This makes neutron-induced nuclear reactions especially important in fission reactors.

History → physics connection

The discovery of the nucleus explained where nuclear energy is stored; the discovery of the neutron provided a practical way to trigger nuclear reactions; the discovery of fission showed that heavy nuclei can split and release energy.

02
FISSION PHYSICS

Break the nucleus, follow the energy

The key ideas are neutron absorption, nuclear instability, binding energy, mass-energy conversion and chain reactions.

①

Neutron absorption

A U-235 nucleus absorbs a neutron and forms an excited compound nucleus. The extra energy can make the nucleus unstable.

②

Nuclear deformation

The excited nucleus can stretch. Short-range nuclear attraction competes with electrostatic repulsion between protons.

③

Fission products

The heavy nucleus splits into two main fragments and usually releases several neutrons and gamma radiation.

④

Energy release

The fragments carry large kinetic energy. Their motion is converted into heat through collisions in the fuel and surrounding materials.

SIMPLIFIED EXAMPLE

²³⁵U + ¹n → fission fragments + 2–3n + energy

The exact fission fragments are not always the same. The important idea is conservation of nucleon number, charge and energy across the reaction.

E = Δmc²A small decrease in total mass-energy can correspond to a large released energy because c² is very large.
BINDING ENERGY

Why heavy nuclei can release energy by splitting

Medium-mass nuclei are generally more tightly bound per nucleon than very heavy nuclei. Fission moves the products toward a more tightly bound region, so the final system can have lower mass-energy. The difference is released as kinetic energy and radiation.

k < 1

Subcritical

Fewer neutrons successfully continue the next generation, so the chain reaction decreases.

k ≈ 1

Critical

Each generation produces enough successful fissions to replace itself. Reactor power can remain steady.

k > 1

Supercritical

The neutron population grows from generation to generation, so power rises.

Moderation

Fast neutrons lose kinetic energy through collisions with moderator nuclei. In thermal reactors this increases the chance that a neutron will cause U-235 fission.

Reactivity control

Control rods absorb neutrons. Inserting them farther removes more neutrons from the chain reaction; withdrawing them generally increases reactivity.

Heat removal

Even after fission stops, radioactive fission products continue to release decay heat. Cooling remains necessary after shutdown.

PWR

Pressurized Water Reactor

Primary water is kept under high pressure so it does not boil in the core. It transfers heat to a separate secondary loop that produces steam.

BWR

Boiling Water Reactor

Water boils directly in the reactor vessel. The steam travels to the turbine, then is condensed and returned to the cycle.

Accidents as physics lessons

Studying accidents helps us understand feedback, cooling, decay heat and defence in depth.

Positive feedback can amplify a disturbance

Under the accident conditions in the RBMK reactor, increasing steam voids could increase reactivity. More reactivity increased power, producing more boiling and more steam voids. This is an example of dangerous positive feedback.

voids ↑ → reactivity ↑ → power ↑ → boiling ↑

Physics lesson: feedback coefficients matter because they determine whether a change naturally damps out or grows.

🌊 ⚡✕ 🏭

Shutdown does not mean zero heat

The reactors shut down after the earthquake, but radioactive fission products continued producing decay heat. Tsunami flooding caused severe loss of electrical power and cooling capability, making heat removal difficult.

shutdown ≠ zero heat → cooling still required

Physics lesson: residual heat must be removed even when the chain reaction has stopped.

🛡️

Defence in depth

Nuclear safety uses multiple layers: reliable design, automatic protection, cooling, containment, monitoring, procedures and emergency response. Safety should not depend on one component working perfectly.

05
TOMORROW'S REACTORS

What may come after today's reactors?

Advanced reactor research explores smaller units, different coolants, fast-neutron systems and new fuel forms.

SMR

Small Modular Reactors

Smaller reactor units designed around modular manufacturing and flexible deployment. Some concepts emphasize passive safety and simpler systems.

MSR

Molten Salt Reactors

Use molten salts as coolant; in some designs, fuel is dissolved in the salt. High-temperature operation is one attraction of the concept.

FAST

Fast Reactors

Operate with fast neutrons instead of heavily moderated thermal neutrons. Sodium, lead or other coolants may be used depending on the design.

HTGR

High-Temperature Gas Reactors

Use gas cooling and high-temperature fuel systems. Besides electricity, the high outlet temperature may support industrial heat applications.

☀️
BEYOND FISSION

Fusion joins light nuclei instead of splitting heavy ones

Fusion powers the Sun. On Earth, researchers create extremely hot plasma so light nuclei can overcome electrostatic repulsion and fuse. Commercial fusion power is still a research and engineering challenge.
